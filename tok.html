Theory of Knowledge

One question about knowledge for each subtopic, to discuss in class or use in TOK.

SL path

Unit 1 Body Systems

SubtopicQuestion about knowledgeWhere it connects
B3.1 (Animal)Who decides what counts as normal?Reference values for lung function were long adjusted by race, a practice now widely challenged. Standards that look objective can carry social assumptions.
B3.2 (Animal)Can we be justified in believing something we cannot yet observe?Harvey's theory predicted vessels nobody had seen. Compare how much confidence a prediction deserves before and after direct observation.
C2.2How far can findings from one species be applied to others?Much of what we know about human neurons began with squid. What must be true for that extrapolation to be reasonable?
C3.1Can a whole system be understood by studying its parts?Biologists often isolate one organ or signal. Consider what integration between systems might be missed when studied that way.
D3.3What limits should there be on how knowledge is obtained?The insulin work relied on experiments on dogs. Consider how the benefits of knowledge should be weighed against the way it was gained.
C3.2Why might good evidence be rejected?Semmelweis's data were strong, but they conflicted with accepted medical ideas and he had no mechanism to offer. What makes a community ready to accept new knowledge?
D3.1 (Human)Should what we can do decide what we should do?Reproductive technology raises questions that data alone cannot answer. Consider how science and ethics each contribute to these decisions.

Unit 2 Plant Systems

SubtopicQuestion about knowledgeWhere it connects
B3.1 (Plant)How reliably can we know about a past nobody observed?Fossil stomata are indirect evidence. Consider what assumptions link a modern pattern to a time millions of years ago.
B3.2 (Plant)How does the act of measuring affect what is measured?Probing xylem can break the water column. Consider other areas of knowledge where observing something changes it.
D3.1 (Plant)How much does a successful prediction strengthen a theory?Compare a prediction made in advance with an explanation offered afterwards. Why might the first count for more?

Unit 3 Genetics

SubtopicQuestion about knowledgeWhere it connects
D1.1What makes an experiment elegant?The design made each hypothesis predict a different result. Consider whether a simple, decisive design is a kind of beauty, and whether beauty matters.
D1.2How does language shape understanding?We say the genetic code is read, copied and translated. Consider where these metaphors help, and where they may mislead.
D1.3What does it mean to call an event random?Mutation is called random, yet some sites mutate more often and mutagens raise the rate. Is randomness a feature of nature, or a statement about what we can predict?
D2.1Do our tools reveal reality or shape it?Stains and fixatives alter cells before we see them, and cells on a slide are dead. How do we know that what we see is not an artefact?
D3.2What should we do with results that look too good?Statistician R. A. Fisher argued that Mendel's data fit the ratios suspiciously well. Consider what this means for trust, and for how data are recorded.

Unit 4 Biomolecules

SubtopicQuestion about knowledgeWhere it connects
A1.1How does science correct its own mistakes?Researchers around the world tested the claim and rejected it. Consider what this says about the value of replication.
A1.2Who gets the credit in collaborative knowledge?Franklin's contribution was long under-recognised. Consider how credit is assigned, and who may be left out.
B1.1How should we act when evidence is uncertain?Public advice must be given before science is settled. Consider how confidence should be communicated when evidence may change.
B1.2How can new techniques change the questions we ask?Before sequencing, some doubted that proteins had a defined order. Consider how methods decide which questions can be answered.
C1.1Is a simpler model ever better than a more accurate one?Lock and key is still taught first. Consider when usefulness and accuracy pull in different directions.
C1.2How do comparisons help us to know something new?The experiment treated life as chemistry. Consider what a comparison can show, and where it can mislead.
C1.3How can careful experiments lead to wrong conclusions?He could not test for what he had not thought of. Consider how existing ideas limit how we interpret results.

Unit 5 Cells

SubtopicQuestion about knowledgeWhere it connects
A2.2Is seeing through an instrument really seeing?We trust a microscope image without seeing the object directly. What gives instruments that authority?
B2.1If models get replaced, in what sense are they knowledge?A model can be useful and later be shown to be wrong. Consider whether today's fluid mosaic model is true, or simply the best available.
B2.2Why is it stronger when independent methods agree?Seeing a structure and isolating its function are different kinds of evidence. Consider why agreement between methods increases confidence.
B2.3Can ethics change when technology changes?New methods removed one objection while raising other questions. Consider whether ethical conclusions should depend on what is technically possible.
D2.3How do we justify claims about things we cannot observe?Mass changes are the data, and osmosis is the explanation. Consider how far an inference goes beyond what was measured.

Unit 6 Biodiversity

SubtopicQuestion about knowledgeWhere it connects
A3.1How can expertise make errors last?Some researchers doubted their own counts because 48 was the accepted number. Consider how authority can affect what people report.
A4.1What does theory mean in science?In everyday speech, a theory is a guess. Consider how scientific usage differs, and why that matters for public understanding.
A4.2Can science tell us what is worth saving?Prioritising species combines data with values. Consider which parts of the decision science can and cannot settle.
D4.1How do we test theories about long-term processes?Evolution usually acts over long periods. Consider how far short-term observations can support claims about deep time.
B4.2How well do laboratory results apply to the natural world?A flask is far simpler than an ecosystem. Consider what is gained and lost by simplifying a system to study it.

Unit 7 Ecosystems

SubtopicQuestion about knowledgeWhere it connects
B4.1Are categories discovered or invented?Nature has no sharp borders between biomes. Consider whether our categories describe nature or simply help us to think about it.
C4.1Can an estimate count as knowledge?A population estimate is never exact. Consider how stating uncertainty affects the strength of a claim.
C4.2Why does long-term data have special value?Nobody could know in advance how much the record would matter. Consider how knowledge depends on funding work with no immediate payoff.
D4.2Is removing part of a system a fair way to learn about it?Manipulating nature can reveal hidden links, but it also interferes. Consider when knowledge justifies that interference.
D4.3What role does consensus play in knowledge?Consensus is not proof, yet it is meaningful. Consider how non-experts should decide whom to trust on complex issues.

AHL path

Unit H1 Body Systems

SubtopicQuestion about knowledgeWhere it connects
B3.3What does independent discovery tell us about knowledge?Two teams reached the same idea at the same time. Consider what this suggests about how knowledge develops.
C2.1How do metaphors help and limit explanation?We describe signalling using messengers and receptors. Consider what these human images add, and what they might hide.
B3.1How does naming shape what we remember?Discoveries are often credited to one person. Consider how this affects our picture of how science works.
B3.2How do we learn to read data from an instrument?An ECG trace is meaningless without training. Consider what makes expert interpretation reliable, and when experts might disagree.
C2.2What is the role of intuition in science?The idea may have come from a dream, but the evidence came from an experiment. Consider how discovery and justification differ.
D3.3How should scarce medical resources be allocated?Dialysis and transplants are not available to all who need them. Consider which kinds of knowledge should inform these decisions.
D3.1Why can correlation mislead us?Women who chose HRT may have differed in other ways. Consider why the design of a study affects how far we can trust its conclusions.

Unit H2 Plant Systems

SubtopicQuestion about knowledgeWhere it connects
B3.2 (Plant)What makes a model the best explanation we have?The pressure-flow model is widely accepted but still debated in its details. Consider what kind of evidence would make scientists change it.
C3.1How does knowledge build across generations?Each step depended on the one before. Consider how a question can stay productive for decades.
D2.3What does it mean to measure something abstract?Water potential is a concept defined by a calculation. Consider whether it is a real property or a useful construct.

Unit H3 Genetics

SubtopicQuestion about knowledgeWhere it connects
D2.2What can natural experiments tell us?Researchers cannot ethically create a famine. Consider the strengths and limits of evidence from events that were never designed as experiments.
D1.1What is the role of puzzles in science?A contradiction between models can lead to discovery. Consider why anomalies can be more useful than confirming results.
D1.2What happens when a simple rule meets its exceptions?Consider when an exception should change a rule, and when it should only qualify it.
D1.3Who should decide how powerful knowledge is used?Consider the roles of scientists, governments and the public in setting limits on technology.
D2.1Does the value of knowledge justify how it was obtained?HeLa cells have brought huge benefits, but consent was never given. Consider who should benefit from research and who should decide.
D3.2Why is a significance level of 5% used?The chi-squared cut-off is a convention. Consider how an agreed threshold shapes what counts as a real result.

Unit H4 Biomolecules

SubtopicQuestion about knowledgeWhere it connects
A1.1How do we reason from a small sample to a whole?One comet cannot represent them all. Consider how much a single striking measurement should change our view.
A1.2Why is some evidence believed more readily than other evidence?Many expected the genetic material to be protein. Consider how prior expectations affect the weight given to results.
B1.2Is predicting the same as understanding?An AI system can predict a structure without giving a human explanation. Consider whether that counts as knowing why.
C1.1How do simplified mathematical models produce knowledge?The model assumes conditions rarely found in cells. Consider how something known to be simplified can still be reliable.
C1.2How do paradigm shifts happen in science?Researchers had been searching for a chemical intermediate. Consider how an expected answer can make a different one harder to accept.
C1.3How do new technologies create new knowledge?Radioactive tracers made a hidden pathway visible. Consider how much scientific knowledge depends on the tools available.

Unit H5 Cells

SubtopicQuestion about knowledgeWhere it connects
A2.1How can we know about events that cannot be repeated?No experiment can replay the origin of life. Consider the difference between showing something could happen and showing it did.
A2.3Is the boundary of life a discovery or a decision?Whether viruses are alive depends on how we define life. Consider what kind of question this is.
A2.2What helps an unpopular idea to gain acceptance?Consider the roles of new evidence, new methods and the persistence of individuals when knowledge changes.
B2.1Can one result settle a question?A single clear experiment can be very persuasive. Consider what else would be needed before a scientific community accepts a claim.
B2.2How do we separate reality from artefact?Consider what kinds of evidence persuade us that a structure is real, and not a product of the method used to see it.
B2.3Can observation be free from theory?What we expect can influence what we see. Consider what safeguards help scientists to observe more objectively.

Unit H6 Biodiversity

SubtopicQuestion about knowledgeWhere it connects
A3.2Do classifications describe nature or organize our thinking?The tree of life changed when the data changed. Consider what this implies about taxonomy as knowledge.
A3.1Is a species a natural kind or a human label?Different species concepts can give different answers. Consider what this means for the claim that a new species has been found.
A4.1How can historical sciences be tested?Most speciation happened long before anyone could watch. Consider how evolutionary biology's methods compare with those of history.
D4.1How can a model known to be unrealistic be useful?Its assumptions are almost never met in nature. Consider how departures from an ideal can themselves be informative.
