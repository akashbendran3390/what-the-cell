CSP: Survival on a Tropical Island

Teams of ESS, Biology, Chemistry and Physics students designed a colony that could last for generations on a real island, then defended it to a sceptical audience.

Run October 2026, Grades 11 and 12, about 100 students in 20 teams
Length Two and a half days off timetable (about 20 hours)
Subjects ESS, Biology, Chemistry and Physics in every team of 4 or 5
Final product A 15-minute colony pitch, then a 5-minute challenge round
Assessed Only each student’s 100-word reflection. The pitch is not graded

⇩ Student guide (PDF)

The scenario

A fast-moving virus has collapsed global civilisation. Your team survived and does not carry the disease. You have one small boat and one chance to collect supplies before leaving the mainland for good.

Your mission: choose a real island in Southeast Asia or the Pacific, research it, and design a colony that can support your community and the generations born there, for ever.

The constraints:

  • Everything must fit on a boat about 6 m long, carrying 1,500 to 2,000 kg in total.
  • The community must be big enough to survive genetically and socially.
  • The colony must not destroy the island’s ecology to survive.
  • The design must cover eight systems: food, water, shelter, energy, health, waste, governance and population.

Why it works as a CSP: no subject can design the colony alone. A shelter built without knowing the soil chemistry fails; a farm planned without the water cycle fails. The scenario forces the four sciences to work together.

Who does what

Subject Role in the colony Leads on
ESS Sustainability and land-use planner Ecosystems, carrying capacity, land use, waste, long-term environmental risk
Biology Food, health and population officer Edible species, food production, disease prevention, sanitation, minimum viable population
Chemistry Water, soil and materials specialist Finding and purifying fresh water, soil chemistry, materials, chemical safety
Physics Infrastructure and energy engineer Shelter design, renewable energy, moving water, building with natural materials

The biology at the heart of it

Biology students lead some of the hardest questions in the project:

  • How many people do we need? Too few and inbreeding becomes a risk within a few generations; too few also means not enough hands to farm, build and care for the sick. Too many and the island’s carrying capacity is exceeded. Teams justified a number, usually between 80 and 150, with evidence from at least two subjects. Links: D4.1 Natural selection, C4.1 Populations and communities.
  • What will we eat? Which crops suit the climate and soil, which give enough energy and nutrients, and what can be fished or foraged before the first harvest. Links: C4.2 Transfers of energy and matter, B4.2 Ecological niches.
  • How do we stay healthy? Likely diseases in a tropical setting, safe water and sanitation, and what to do when medicine runs out. Link: C3.2 Defence against disease.
  • How do we avoid wrecking the island? Which species and habitats must be protected because the colony depends on them. Link: A4.2 Conservation of biodiversity.

How the two and a half days ran

When What teams did Deadline
Day 1 Scenario briefing and a map activity; meet the team; choose an island using Google Earth, GBIF and the IUCN Red List; research by subject; the supplies debate Island research dossier (end of day)
Day 2 Final supplies list, with a reason for every item and the weight checked; design each system; draw a systems diagram linking them; find gaps; build the presentation Annotated supplies manifest (midday); systems diagram and at least 3 interdisciplinary links (end of day)
Day 3 (morning) Final run-through, then the colony pitches and challenge rounds The pitch
Day 3 (end) Whole-group debrief; individual reflections 100-word reflection

Throughout, each team kept a shared colony design journal: every decision, every rejected idea and why.

Example islands

Teams could pick any island with enough data. Suggestions included Con Dao and Phu Quoc (Vietnam), Koh Rong (Cambodia), Pulau Tioman (Malaysia), the Banda Islands and Nias (Indonesia), Romblon (Philippines) and Koh Lanta (Thailand).

What went on the boat

Every item needed a written reason, and anything the island could provide was left behind. The categories teams argued over:

  • seeds and starter crops (energy-dense or nutritionally varied?)
  • medicines and medical tools (what can never be made on the island?)
  • hand tools, fishing gear and navigation equipment
  • solar panels, small wind turbines, batteries and wiring
  • water filters and purification chemicals
  • books on medicine, farming, engineering and ecology
  • breeding animals (which ones, and what will they eat?)
  • building materials the island cannot supply

The colony pitch

The audience played prospective colonists deciding which colony to join. Each 15-minute pitch covered the island profile, community size and governance, food, water and soil, shelter and energy, health and sanitation, the top ten supplies, the links between subjects, long-term sustainability, and the biggest risk with a plan to manage it.

The best pitches showed rather than told: a colony map, a water-system diagram, or a chart of food produced against energy needed.

Challenge round questions

  • What do you eat in week 1, before any crops are ready?
  • Walk us through how water gets from the source to a safe drink.
  • Someone breaks a leg in month 2. What happens?
  • Two groups disagree about sharing food. How is it settled?
  • Your solar panels wear out in 10 to 15 years. Then what?
  • Your population has grown to 300 after 20 years. Is the island still viable?
  • A cyclone destroys half the shelters and crops in year 2. Does the colony survive?

Not knowing an answer was allowed: teams said what they would research next.

Reflection prompts used

  • What was it like combining your science with three other subjects?
  • Where did disagreements come from, and how were they resolved?
  • What did you learn about sustainability that your own subject alone could not teach you?
  • What would you do differently, in the design or in how the team worked?

Students were asked to describe one real moment, not to summarise what the team produced.

Running it again: tips from the first run

  • Form mixed teams in advance, with every subject in each team, and balance Grade 11 and Grade 12.
  • Give each staff member a cluster of teams to circulate around; teachers ask questions rather than give answers.
  • Check midday and end-of-day deadlines in person: they keep teams moving.
  • Make sure teams leave Day 2 with a nearly complete pitch, because Day 3 is short.
  • At the end, ask the whole group: which colony would you actually join, and why?
