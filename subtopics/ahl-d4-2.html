Succession

D4.2.11

What drives succession

p. 814

Succession is a directional change in the species of a community over time, driven by abiotic changes such as water, temperature and soil, and by biotic changes such as competition. Primary succession starts without soil; secondary succession starts where soil remains.

D4.2.12

Primary succession

pp. 815–816

Pioneers such as lichens begin to form soil, which lets larger plants root. Plant size, primary production, species diversity, food-web complexity and nutrient cycling all increase.

D4.2.13

Cyclical succession

p. 817

Some ecosystems repeat a cycle of communities with no fixed climax. On some New Zealand rocky shores, barnacles, algae and then mussels settle in turn, and when the mussels detach the rock is bare again.

D4.2.14

Climax and arrested succession

pp. 818–819

A climax community is stable and in balance with its climate and soil. Human activity can arrest succession: livestock eat tree seedlings, and draining wetlands removes the waterlogged conditions their species need.

Practical

Succession in a jar

Lab20 min, then 2 weeksD4.2.11

You will need: Hay or dried grass, pond water, clear jars, microscope, slides

  1. Set up jars of hay and pond water, loosely covered
  2. Sample every few days and view under a microscope
  3. Record which types of organism appear and when
  4. Describe the changes, and relate them to succession

Safety: Wash hands after handling. Keep lids loose, and dispose of it all according to school procedures.

Carry this out with your teacher's supervision and follow your school's lab rules.

Nature of science and TOK

Nature of science

Hypotheses

In the early 1900s, Clements saw succession as ordered and predictable, while Gleason emphasised chance. Each view still shapes ecology.

Theory of knowledge

What happens when experts interpret the same evidence differently?

Both drew on real plant communities. Consider how scientists decide between competing interpretations.
