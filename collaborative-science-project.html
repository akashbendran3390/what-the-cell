Collaborative Sciences Project

A short team project with students from other sciences: what it is, the three stages, biology project ideas and help with your reflection.

What it is A team project in which students from different DP science subjects tackle a real-world problem together, using scientific methods
Who does it Every student taking a DP science, usually in Year 1
Time At least 10 hours. Some schools run it over two days off timetable; others spread it over several weeks
Marks None. It is a course requirement, not an assessed task, and the process matters more than the product
What you hand in A short personal reflection of about 100 words, which your school keeps as evidence

These facts come from the IB Collaborative sciences project guide. Your school decides the dates, the teams and the theme, so check the details with your teacher.

The point of the CSP is to experience how real science works: people from different fields solving one problem together. You will not solve climate change in 10 hours, and nobody expects you to. What counts is how well your team inquires, collaborates and communicates.

What every CSP includes

  • Teamwork across subjects. Ideally your team mixes biology with chemistry, physics, computer science, ESS, SEHS or design technology.
  • A local problem with a global link. Pick an issue you can study near you that connects to a bigger issue, often one of the UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs).
  • Inquiry and problem-solving. You ask a question, collect evidence or build a model, and propose a solution.
  • Communication. You share your work with others, for example as a poster, short talk, video, podcast, web page or exhibition.
  • Reflection. You think about how the team worked, not just what it made.

The three stages

1. Plan

  • Meet your team, share what each subject can bring, and agree on roles (for example coordinator, data lead, communicator, recorder).
  • Choose an issue that is local, specific and possible in the time you have.
  • Write a short proposal: your aim, a timeline, an outline of the method, an equipment list and a risk assessment. Add consent forms if people will take part.
  • Start a shared process journal straight away: notes, photos, decisions and who did what.

2. Act

  • Carry out the investigation, build the model or make the product.
  • Check in with your mentor at agreed points, and share progress with other teams.
  • Keep the journal going. Record what went wrong and how you fixed it; this is the best material for your reflection.
  • Prepare to present: what you aimed to do, what you did and found, the local and global links, and one or two successes and challenges.

3. Reflect

  • Talk as a team about what helped and what got in the way of working together.
  • Write your own reflection of about 100 words. Write about the experience and what you learned, not a list of tasks.

Biology project ideas

Each idea links a local problem to a global goal and needs at least one other science. Adapt them to your school and area.

Global goal Project idea How biology helps Other sciences
SDG 3 Good health Map hand-washing habits around school and test how well different hand-washing methods remove bacteria Culturing and counting bacteria safely, immune defence Chemistry (soaps), computer science (survey and data)
SDG 6 Clean water Monitor the water quality of a local stream, pond or water tank Indicator invertebrates, algal growth, eutrophication Chemistry (nitrate, phosphate, oxygen), physics (sensors)
SDG 11 Sustainable cities Measure how much cooler shaded or planted spots on campus are than bare ones Transpiration, plant cover, biodiversity counts Physics (heat and temperature), ESS
SDG 12 Responsible consumption Turn canteen food waste into compost and compare how fast different mixes break down Decomposers, nutrient cycling Chemistry (pH, carbon to nitrogen ratio), ESS
SDG 13 Climate action Estimate how much carbon the trees on the school grounds store Photosynthesis, carbon cycle Physics and maths (tree measurements), computer science (model)
SDG 14 Life below water Count and sort microplastics in sand or water samples from a nearby shore or drain Food chains, bioaccumulation Chemistry (plastic types), ESS
SDG 15 Life on land Run a pollinator or bird count and design a planting plan to improve it Biodiversity indices, interdependence Design technology (nest boxes, planters), ESS

Tips for working well as a team

  • Agree how you will decide. Vote, take turns, or let the role-holder decide; settle it before you start.
  • Split the work, share the thinking. Everyone should understand the whole project, even if they lead one part.
  • Keep one shared folder and give files clear names. Back up photos and data the same day.
  • Deal with problems early. If someone is missing or stuck, raise it with the team and your mentor straight away.
  • Credit sources and each other. Cite what you use, and say who did what in the journal.

Writing your 100-word reflection

Use one or two of these prompts. Be honest and specific.

  • What was one moment when the team worked really well together? Why did it work?
  • What was the hardest part of collaborating or communicating, and what did you do about it?
  • What did you learn from a teammate who studies a different science?
  • How did your project connect a local issue to a global one?
  • What would you do differently in your next team project?
  • Which TOK question did the project raise for you? For example: how do scientists from different fields agree on what counts as evidence?
