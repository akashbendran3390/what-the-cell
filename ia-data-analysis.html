Stage 6: Descriptive statistics

Week 6 · Begins with the in-class mentor session on data analysis.

Descriptive statistics summarise your data so a reader can see what it shows; they do not test your hypothesis (that is Stage 8). Choose the statistics that fit your question and data, not what a classmate did.

For every calculation, write in your report:

  1. Why you did it.

  2. Where to find the results (“See Table 4 for the mean and standard deviation at each sucrose concentration.”).

  3. One worked example using your data: the formula with your numbers, or a clear, cited screenshot of a spreadsheet or online calculator, labelled with the level it uses.

Round calculated values to the same number of decimal places as the least precise raw value.

Step 1: Preliminary calculations (if needed)

Do these for each trial, before averaging.

Calculation Formula Biology example
Total change final − initial Change in height of seedlings
Percentage change (final − initial) ÷ initial × 100 Change in mass of potato cylinders in osmosis
Rate change ÷ time, or 1/t Volume of O₂ per minute; 1/time for a colour change
Other e.g. Simpson’s diversity index, percentage cover Field studies

Step 2: What kind of study is it?

  • Levels of an independent variable with repeated trials → Step 3.

  • Paired data, no repeats (e.g. light intensity and leaf area for 30 plants) → Step 6.

  • Unique data (presence/absence in quadrats, DNA or protein sequences) → Step 7.

Step 3: Check the shape of the data (skew)

Calculate skew for each level using the SKEW function in Excel or Google Sheets, or a cited online calculator.

Absolute skew Meaning Use
0 to 0.5 Approximately symmetrical Mean
0.5 to 1.0 Slightly skewed Mean
1.0 to 2.0 Moderately skewed, acceptable Mean
Above 2.0 Strongly skewed Median

Negative skew = more values above the mean; positive skew = more values below. Report direction and strength for each level. Check outliers with the 1.5 × IQR rule; justify keeping or removing them and, if it matters, show results both ways.

Step 6: Correlation studies

  1. Draw a scatter graph.

  2. Linear → Pearson’s r. Curved but always rising or always falling (monotonic) → Spearman’s rank rₛ.

  3. Calculate with all data pairs (=PEARSON( ) or a cited calculator).

  4. Describe direction (positive or negative) and strength: up to 0.5 weak; 0.5–0.8 moderate; 0.8–1.0 strong.

Step 7: Unique data

Write a paragraph and build a summary table describing the data (for example, the number of quadrats in which each species was present, or sequence similarity percentages).

Stage 8: Inferential statistics

Week 7

Inferential statistics tell you whether your results are likely to be real or could have happened by chance. Choose the test that fits your data; running a test that does not fit loses marks.

Step 1: Key ideas

  • Significant: very unlikely to be due to chance. Not significant: could be due to chance.

  • p-value: the probability that results like yours happen by chance alone (p = 0.025 means a 2.5% chance).

  • Significance level: the cut-off used in biology is 0.05. If p < 0.05, the result is significant.

  • Significance is more likely with a large difference between groups, small spread within groups, and a large sample.

In your report, define an inferential test, significant and not significant, and state your significance level, with a citation.

Step 2: Choose your test from your graph

Your graph and data Test Statistic
Bar chart of means ± SD, 2 groups, unrelated samples Unpaired t-test (two-tailed) t
Bar chart of means ± SD, 2 groups, same samples before and after Paired t-test t
Means ± SD, 3 or more groups One-way ANOVA + Tukey post-hoc F
Box and whisker of medians (skewed data) Kruskal–Wallis + Dunn post-hoc H
Stacked bar or pie chart of counts; phenotype ratios; species association Chi-squared χ²
Scatter or line of means, linear trendline Pearson’s correlation r
Scatter or line of means, curved but monotonic Spearman’s rank correlation rₛ
Line of means, non-monotonic (e.g. enzyme optimum) ANOVA + post-hoc F

Explain why your test suits your data. Chi-squared works only on counts, not percentages or means.

Step 3: Statistical hypotheses

These are different from your research hypothesis. Match the wording to the test.

Test Null hypothesis (H₀) Alternative hypothesis (H₁)
t-test, ANOVA, Kruskal–Wallis There is no significant difference in [DV] between [levels of IV]; any difference may be due to chance. There is a significant difference in [DV] between [levels of IV].
Chi-squared (association) There is no significant association between [species A] and [species B] at [site]. There is a significant association between [species A] and [species B].
Chi-squared (goodness of fit) There is no significant difference between observed and expected [counts/ratio]. There is a significant difference between observed and expected [counts/ratio].
Pearson, Spearman There is no significant correlation between [X] and [Y]. There is a significant correlation between [X] and [Y].

Step 4: Run the test and show working

Use a spreadsheet or a reputable online calculator. Include a clear, captioned, cited screenshot or worked calculation. If ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis is significant, report the post-hoc test to show which groups differ.
