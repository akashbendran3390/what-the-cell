CSP: Mythbusters

Each team chose an everyday myth, tested it with every science in the group, and declared it confirmed or busted.

Run Around 2019, as a Group 4 project (the earlier name for the CSP)
Teams Groups of 5, each student the specialist for their subject (Biology, Chemistry, Physics or ESS)
Shape A planning day in October, an equipment check, two action days in May, then an evaluation day
Final product A poster and an 18 to 20 minute video, presented to classmates

The idea

The TV show MythBusters takes something people believe, tests it properly, and declares it confirmed or busted. Each team did the same: chose a myth, designed an investigation that needed every team member’s science, ran it, and reached a verdict from the evidence.

Why it works as a CSP: everyday myths rarely belong to one subject. Testing whether a drink keeps you awake, or whether a food is safe, needs biology, chemistry and physics working on the same question.

Myths teams tested

  • Radiation from a Wi-Fi router slows the sprouting of soybeans.
  • You can tell how sweet a watermelon is from the sound it makes when tapped.
  • Instant noodles cannot be digested within 30 days.
  • Tea stops you falling asleep.
  • Sprouted potatoes are poisonous to humans.
  • Milk loses its nutrients when heated in a microwave.
  • Cola can be used as a cleaning agent.
  • Optical fingerprint readers can be tricked.
  • Self-heating hot pots cook food safely, as the packaging promises.

Good biology myths have something measurable: germination rate, enzyme activity, bacterial growth, vitamin C content, heart rate or reaction time. Links: C1.1 Enzymes, B1.2 Proteins, C3.2 Defence against disease.

How it ran

Stage What teams did
Planning day (October) Agree a myth and a clear research question, written on a whiteboard; plan the method as a flowchart that shows every subject’s part; request all equipment; write a planning-day reflection
Approval Every subject supervisor and the lab technician signed off the question, the method and the equipment list
Equipment check One period, a few days before the action days, to check that everything had arrived and worked, and to make final requests
Action days (May) Run the experiments; collect, process and analyse data; film and photograph everything; build the poster and video
Evaluation day Each team presented its poster and video to classmates, then wrote a final reflection

The equipment request

Each team submitted one spreadsheet with these columns: item, quantity (with sizes, for example 500 ml beakers), unit price, shipping cost, total cost, supplier link and notes. Sensors and data loggers had to be requested separately.

The teacher’s role

Supervisors acted as lab technicians and questioners: they asked about the investigation, pointed students to sources and assessed the reflections, poster and video. They did not teach the content or keep teams on track. That was the team’s job.

Reflection

Each student wrote four reflections across the project on a shared team page (a Padlet), each at least 250 words, with photos, data and sources. The planning-day prompts were:

  • What is your team’s research question?
  • What could your investigation mean for your own subject, and for the wider world?
  • What expertise will each team member bring?
  • What conflicts might arise, and how will the team handle them?

How it was weighted

At the time, the Group 4 project was marked within each student’s main science: daily reflections 35%, poster 25%, video 25%, and punctuality, attendance and lab clean-up 15%.

Under today’s CSP rules nothing is graded except the individual reflection of about 100 words, and the process matters more than the product. To run Mythbusters as a CSP now, keep the myths, the planning day and the action days, drop the marks, and finish with the short reflection.

Running it again

  • Approve myths early: some need living things (seeds, bacteria) that take days to grow.
  • Check safety and ethics at the planning stage, especially for food, microorganisms and human participants.
  • Ask every team to show on its flowchart where each subject’s evidence comes from.
  • A shorter video (5 minutes) is plenty, and leaves more time for the science.
