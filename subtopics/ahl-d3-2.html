Two genes

D3.2.16

Segregation and independent assortment

pp. 747–748

In meiosis I, homologous chromosomes separate, so each gamete gets one allele of each gene (segregation). Each pair of homologues lines up at random in metaphase I, so genes on different chromosomes are inherited independently (independent assortment). A parent RrYy makes RY, Ry, rY and ry gametes in equal numbers.

D3.2.17

Dihybrid Punnett grids

pp. 748–750

Crossing two double heterozygotes for unlinked genes gives a 9:3:3:1 phenotype ratio. A test cross of a double heterozygote with a double homozygous recessive gives 1:1:1:1.

D3.2.18

Gene loci

pp. 750–751

Databases give each human gene’s locus and product. HBB (beta globin) and HBD (delta globin) lie next to each other on chromosome 11; HBA1 (alpha globin) is on chromosome 16; CFTR is on chromosome 7.

Linkage and statistics

D3.2.19

Linkage

pp. 751–752

Genes on the same chromosome are linked and tend to be inherited together, so they do not assort independently. They are shown with alleles beside vertical lines, one line per homologous chromosome. The closer the loci, the stronger the linkage.

D3.2.20

Recombinants

pp. 752–754

A recombinant has a combination of alleles not found in either parent. With linked genes, recombinants come only from crossing over between the loci, so a test cross gives two large parental classes and two small recombinant classes.

D3.2.21

Chi-squared test

pp. 755–757

The null hypothesis is that observed numbers do not differ significantly from those expected (for example 9:3:3:1). Calculate χ2 = Σ(O − E)2/E, with degrees of freedom = classes − 1. If χ2 is greater than the critical value at p = 0.05 (7.81 for 3 degrees of freedom), reject the null hypothesis; the genes may be linked.

Practical

Dihybrid crosses and chi-squared

Model45 minD3.2.17, D3.2.21

You will need: Two coins per student, Punnett grid template, chi-squared table

  1. Treat each coin as a heterozygous gene, and toss both coins for each parent
  2. Record 80 offspring from a cross of two heterozygotes
  3. Compare the results to the expected 9:3:3:1 ratio
  4. Calculate chi-squared and decide whether the difference is significant

Safety: No practical hazards.

Carry this out with your teacher's supervision and follow your school's lab rules.

Nature of science and TOK

Nature of science

Patterns and trends

Morgan's fruit fly results did not fit independent assortment. In 1913 his student Sturtevant used this to make the first genetic map.

Theory of knowledge

Why is a significance level of 5% used?

The chi-squared cut-off is a convention. Consider how an agreed threshold shapes what counts as a real result.
