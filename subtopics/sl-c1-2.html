C1.2.1

ATP distributes energy within cells

pp. 405–411

ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is a nucleotide. It is soluble, so it moves easily around a cell. It cannot pass through membranes, so it stays where it is needed. It also releases a useful quantity of energy, enough for many tasks.

C1.2.2

What ATP is used for

pp. 405–411
  • Active transport across membranes
  • Synthesis of macromolecules, which is anabolism
  • Movement of the whole cell, or of components such as chromosomes
C1.2.3

ATP and ADP

pp. 405–411

Hydrolysis of ATP to ADP and phosphate releases energy. Energy from respiration is required to make ATP from ADP and phosphate.

C1.2.4

Respiration as a system for producing ATP

pp. 405–411

Respiration uses the energy that carbon compounds release to produce ATP. Glucose and fatty acids are the main substrates, but many others can be used.

Watch out: respiration is a set of reactions in cells. Gas exchange is the movement of oxygen and carbon dioxide across a surface.

C1.2.6

Variables and rates of respiration

pp. 405–411

Temperature, substrate and oxygen supply all affect the rate. A respirometer measures oxygen uptake: a CO2 absorber removes carbon dioxide, so a drop of liquid moves as oxygen is used. If a drop moves 45 mm in 15 minutes in a tube of 1 mm², the rate is 3 mm³ per minute.

Respiratory quotient in finches

8 marks

The respiratory quotient (RQ) is the volume of carbon dioxide produced divided by the volume of oxygen used. Zebra finches were fasted overnight, then fed either seeds (mostly carbohydrate) or insect larvae (mostly lipid and protein). Their RQ was measured for six hours. Typical RQ values: carbohydrate 1.0, protein 0.8, lipid 0.7.

  1. State the RQ before feeding, and deduce the main respiratory substrate being used. [2]
  2. Compare the RQ of the two groups between 1 and 3 hours after feeding. [2]
  3. Suggest why the RQ of the seed-fed birds rises above 1.0. [1]
  4. A bird produced 3.8 cm3 of carbon dioxide while using 4.2 cm3 of oxygen. Calculate its RQ. [1]
  5. Explain why the RQ of the larva-fed birds stays lower than that of the seed-fed birds. [2]

Temperature and metabolic rate in mice

8 marks

Oxygen consumption of house mice was measured at different air temperatures. Mice were tested alone, or in groups of four that could huddle together.

  1. State the oxygen consumption of single mice at 15 °C. [1]
  2. Calculate how much lower, as a percentage, oxygen consumption is for grouped mice than single mice at 15 °C. [2]
  3. Outline the relationship between air temperature and oxygen consumption from 10 to 30 °C. [1]
  4. Explain why oxygen consumption can be used as a measure of metabolic rate. [2]
  5. Suggest why mice in groups use less oxygen in cold air. [1]
  6. Suggest why oxygen consumption rises again at 35 °C. [1]

Mitochondrial myopathy

7 marks

Mitochondrial myopathy is a group of disorders in which the electron transport chain does not work properly. Thirty patients and thirty healthy people (controls) cycled to exhaustion. Oxygen delivery to the muscles (from blood flow) and oxygen use by the muscle cells were measured at peak exercise. Error bars show ± 1 SD.

  1. Calculate the percentage difference in oxygen use by the patients compared with the controls. [2]
  2. Compare oxygen delivery and oxygen use in the patients. [2]
  3. Explain why patients become tired at low levels of exercise. [2]
  4. Suggest why oxygen delivery is similar in both groups. [1]

Practical

Yeast respiration and temperature

Lab40 minC1.2.6

You will need: Dried yeast, sugar solution, flasks or bottles, balloons, water baths, string and ruler

  1. Add equal yeast and sugar solution to each flask and fit a balloon
  2. Stand flasks at three or more temperatures
  3. Measure balloon circumference every five minutes
  4. Plot the results, and explain what affects the rate of respiration

Safety: Keep water baths below 45 C. Vent flasks at the end and wash hands.

Carry this out with your teacher's supervision and follow your school's lab rules.

Nature of science

Experiments

In the 1780s Lavoisier and Laplace compared the heat from a guinea pig with burning charcoal, linking respiration to combustion.
