What counts as a species?

A3.1.12

Limits of the species concept

pp. 118–120

The biological species concept depends on interbreeding. Asexual organisms produce clones and never interbreed, so each line could count as a separate species. Bacteria also pass genes sideways by horizontal gene transfer, even between distant groups, so their gene pools are not isolated.

A3.1.13

Chromosome number

p. 120

Members of a species share one chromosome number. A hybrid of parents with different numbers, such as a mule (63), has chromosomes that cannot all pair in meiosis, so it is usually infertile.

Identifying species

A3.1.14

Dichotomous keys

pp. 121–123

A key is a series of couplets: pairs of opposite statements about one feature. Use features that are easy to see and do not vary, and check that every route ends at one species. Test your key by swapping it with another group.

A3.1.15

eDNA and barcodes

p. 124

Organisms shed DNA into water, soil and air. Barcode regions, short standard sections that differ between species, are copied by PCR, sequenced and matched to a database. This shows quickly which species are present, but not how many individuals there are.

Practical

Making a dichotomous key

Field45 minA3.1.14

You will need: Six to eight local plants or leaves, photographs, ruler

  1. Collect or photograph local specimens
  2. Choose a visible feature that divides them into two groups
  3. Keep dividing until each specimen is identified
  4. Test your key on a classmate, and improve it

Safety: Avoid plants that irritate skin, and do not pick protected plants. Wash hands afterwards.

Carry this out with your teacher's supervision and follow your school's lab rules.
