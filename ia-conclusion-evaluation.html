Stage 10: Write the conclusion

Week 8 · Toddle Task 6 (part A) · Begins with the in-class session ranking five sample conclusions.

The conclusion answers your research question from your analysis and explains it with biology.

Level What it looks like Max
State A brief answer with no detail 2/6
Describe A detailed account of the pattern, but no reasons why 4/6
Justify Data and biological reasons for why it happened 6/6

Component 1: The conclusion statement

  • Answer the research question directly, describing the relationship from your data.

  • State how far your research hypothesis is supported: fully, partly or not at all.

  • Be cautious: the data suggests, indicates, supports. Never proves. Do not generalise beyond your organism, range or conditions.

  • Correlation studies: discuss correlation versus causation.

Component 2: Support from your analysis

  • Quote specific values: means, differences between levels (marginal or pronounced), comparison with a control.

  • Describe features of the data: optimum, plateau or saturation point, threshold, lag or exponential phase, inflection point, isotonic point (osmosis), positive or negative and monotonic or not.

  • Use spread: narrow SD suggests high precision; wide SD suggests natural variation or error; compare error-bar sizes and overlap.

  • Use the inferential test: did you reject the null hypothesis, and what does that say about chance?

Component 3: Uncertainty and error analysis

  • Systematic errors shift all values one way and affect accuracy (for example, a balance reading 0.02 g high; blotting potato cylinders more on one side).

  • Random errors scatter values and affect precision (for example, natural variation between leaves; reaction time when timing).

  • Do not list careless mistakes (“I spilled some solution”) as errors.

  • Judge how measurement uncertainty, variation (outliers, skew, SD, IQR, R²) and the p-value affect your confidence in the conclusion.

Component 4: Biological context

  • Restate the biological phenomenon you investigated.

  • Explain the biological processes that produced your results (for example, collision frequency and active-site denaturation; water potential gradients; photon capture by photosystem II).

  • Explain the variability using biology (for example, genetic variation between individual plants).

  • If results were unexpected, explain the process you expected and suggest why it did not occur.

  • Compare with published studies or accepted values, with citations; where an accepted value exists, calculate percentage error: |experimental − accepted| ÷ accepted × 100.

Worked example

The effect of light intensity on the rate of photosynthesis in Elodea.

As light intensity increased from 500 to 2,000 lux, the mean volume of oxygen produced rose from 4.2 cm³ to 18.6 cm³, a strong positive correlation (Pearson’s r = 0.82). The linear trendline in Figure 7 fits the data well (R² = 0.67). The Pearson’s test gave p = 0.02, below 0.05, so the relationship is statistically significant and unlikely to be due to chance. This supports the hypothesis that oxygen production increases with light intensity. More photons are available at higher intensity, activating more electrons in photosystem II and accelerating the photolysis of water, which releases oxygen (Figure 10).

Every claim has a number, a statistic or a biological reason.

Conclusion frame

1. Answer: The data suggests that as [IV] increased from [x] to [y], [DV] [increased/decreased/levelled off] from [value] to [value].
2. Hypothesis: This [supports / partly supports / does not support] my hypothesis that […].
3. Pattern: [size of change, optimum, plateau, threshold, overlap of error bars].
4. Significance: The [test] gave [statistic] and p = [value], so the result [is/is not] significant.
5. Uncertainty: [Systematic/random errors, SD, R²] mean my confidence is [high/moderate/low] because […].
6. Biology: This is explained by [mechanism] (citation).
7. Comparison: My result [agrees/differs] with [published study or value] (percentage error [z]% if relevant), because […].

Stage 11: Write the evaluation

Week 8 · Toddle Task 6 (part B) · Begins with the in-class session ranking five sample evaluations.

Errors vs weaknesses. Errors (in your conclusion) are deviations from the true value during measurement. Weaknesses (in your evaluation) are flaws in the design of the investigation. They are not careless mistakes or hypothetical events with no evidence.

Level What it looks like Max
State Generic weaknesses and improvements 2/6
Describe Specific weaknesses with relevant improvements 4/6
Explain The relative impact of each specific weakness, with improvements explained 6/6

Component 1: Identify methodological weaknesses (aim for at least three)

  • Sample size: 5–14 trials is very small, 15–30 small, over 30 large. Small samples hide trends and magnify anomalies, especially with variable biological material.

  • Levels and range: could you see the full biological trend (for example, the optimum or the isotonic point)? What would extra levels have shown?

  • Control of variables: which factors fluctuated (temperature, light, age or source of organisms, tissue thickness)?

  • Apparatus precision: was it sensitive enough? (Measuring 1 cm³ with a 100 cm³ cylinder is a limitation.)

  • Duration: was there enough time for the biological response (growth, osmotic equilibrium, culture growth)?

  • Data source: for databases and simulations, how reliable is it?

Support each weakness with evidence from your data: an unexpected trend, inconsistent replicates, a large SD at one level, an outlier.

Component 2: Evaluate the impact

  • Explain how it affected your ability to draw a conclusion.

  • Judge it major (could change the conclusion) or minor (added some variability).

  • Say whether it pushed results higher or lower, or increased scatter.

  • Rank your weaknesses by impact.

Component 3: Realistic improvements

  • One improvement per weakness, directly targeting it.

  • Specific: not “better equipment” but “a digital pH probe (± 0.01) instead of universal indicator paper” or “a cork borer and scalpel guide to cut cylinders to 2.0 cm”.

  • Feasible in a school laboratory or field site.

  • Explain how it would reduce error, increase precision or improve validity.

Write confidently: avoid “I may have…” or “it could have…”.

Worked example

Weakness Impact Improvement
The water bath was set to 30 °C, but the hot plate cycled on and off, so the temperature fluctuated between 25 °C and 35 °C during each 10-minute trial. Major. Enzyme activity in yeast respiration is highly temperature-sensitive, so the fluctuations caused inconsistent respiration rates between trials. This explains the large SD at 30 °C and makes it hard to tell whether the trend was caused by the intended temperature. Replace the hot plate with a thermostatically controlled digital water bath (± 0.5 °C). The stable temperature would reduce variation between trials, giving more precise data and a smaller SD.

Evaluation table to complete

Weakness (design, with evidence from my data) Impact (major/minor, direction, why) Specific, realistic improvement and how it helps
