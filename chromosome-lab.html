Sort a person’s chromosomes and find out what went wrong

A karyogram shows a person’s chromosomes in pairs, from largest to smallest, photographed during cell division. Build one yourself by matching chromosomes by length, centromere position and banding, then decide whether the person is male or female and whether they have the usual number. In the second part, watch how a mistake in meiosis called non-disjunction can give a child three copies of chromosome 21 (Down syndrome).

Key idea

Human body cells have 46 chromosomes: 22 pairs of autosomes and one pair of sex chromosomes (XX in females, XY in males). In each pair, one chromosome came from the mother and one from the father. Karyograms are used to check for an unusual number of chromosomes, for example before birth.

Syllabus: A3.1.7, D3.2.11, D2.1.10. Chromosome sizes and centromere positions are approximately to scale; the band patterns are simplified.

Meiosis in a parent:

What is non-disjunction?

In meiosis I the two chromosomes of each pair should move to opposite poles; in meiosis II the two sister chromatids should separate. If a pair or a pair of chromatids fails to separate (non-disjunction), one gamete gets an extra copy and another gets none. A gamete with two copies of chromosome 21, fused with a normal gamete, gives a zygote with three copies: trisomy 21, which causes Down syndrome.

Syllabus: D2.1.9, D2.1.10

Most cases come from non-disjunction in the mother’s egg cells. Suggest why the chance rises with age.

Possible answer

Egg cells start meiosis before a girl is born and stay paused in meiosis I for many years. The longer they wait, the more likely the proteins that hold the chromosome pairs together are to weaken, so pairs separate wrongly more often.
