Speciation

A4.1.8

Allopatric and sympatric speciation

pp. 151–152

In both, gene pools are isolated and then diverge. In allopatric speciation the populations are separated geographically; in sympatric speciation they share an area but are isolated by behaviour or timing.

A4.1.9

Adaptive radiation

pp. 152–153

One ancestral species diversifies into many that fill different niches, as in Galápagos finches or bromeliads. Because they use different resources, close relatives can coexist without competing, which raises biodiversity.

A4.1.10

Barriers to hybridization

pp. 153–154

Species-specific courtship usually stops animals mating with other species. If hybrids do form, they are often sterile, as mules are, so alleles do not pass between species.

A4.1.11

Abrupt speciation in plants

pp. 154–155

Two species hybridize; the sterile hybrid doubles its chromosomes and becomes fertile. The new polyploid cannot breed successfully with its parents, so it is a new species at once. Many species of Persicaria (knotweeds and smartweeds) arose this way.

Practical

Modelling allopatric speciation

Model35 minA4.1.8

You will need: Two cups, coloured beads as alleles, dice, a different selection rule for each cup

  1. Split a starting bead population into two cups
  2. Apply a different selection rule to each for five generations
  3. Compare the bead frequencies
  4. Discuss how separation could lead to speciation, and how sympatric speciation would differ

Safety: Keep beads off the floor to avoid slips.

Carry this out with your teacher's supervision and follow your school's lab rules.
