How selection works

D4.1.1

Natural selection drives evolution

pp. 779–781

Natural selection operates all the time and has done so for billions of years, producing the biodiversity of life on Earth. Individuals vary, more are born than can survive, and those better adapted leave more offspring.

D4.1.2

Mutation and sexual reproduction create variation

p. 782

Mutation makes new alleles, and sexual reproduction makes new combinations of alleles, through crossing over, random orientation and random fertilization.

D4.1.3

Overproduction and competition

pp. 782–783

More offspring are produced than resources can support, so members of a species compete. Food, water, space and nesting sites can limit a population's carrying capacity.

D4.1.4

Abiotic factors as selection pressures

p. 783

Density-independent factors, such as high or low temperatures, reduce survival at any population density. Individuals with heritable traits suited to the conditions are more likely to survive.

D4.1.5

Differences in adaptation, survival and reproduction

pp. 783–785

This is the basis of selection. Fitness is how likely a genotype is to survive and leave offspring, and it depends on the environment. Competition within a species favours those with higher fitness.

D4.1.6

Traits must be heritable

p. 784

Characteristics acquired in life because of the environment are not encoded in base sequences, so they are not inherited and cannot evolve.

Sexual selection

D4.1.7

Sexual selection

pp. 786–788

Physical and behavioural differences can affect success in attracting a mate, and they can signal fitness. Birds-of-paradise are well-known examples, in which males have exaggerated plumage and displays.

D4.1.8

Modelling selection: Endler's guppies

pp. 788–789

Bright spots attract females, as well as predators. Endler kept guppies under different levels of predation. Spots increased without predators, through sexual selection, and fell with dangerous predators, through natural selection. Selection pressures in the experiment were controlled.

Mate choice in guppies

8 marks

Male guppies (Poecilia reticulata) in Trinidad vary in colour and in behaviour. Males were sorted as bright or dull, and as bold (approaching a predator fish) or timid (swimming away). Forty females were each allowed to choose between males, with and without a predator fish visible in a nearby tank.

Males chosenNo predatorPredator present
Bright and bold1415
Bright and timid135
Dull and bold714
Dull and timid66
  1. State how many females chose dull males when the predator was present. [1]
  2. Calculate the difference in the number of bright, timid males chosen with and without the predator. [1]
  3. Compare the choices made by females with and without the predator. [2]
  4. Suggest reasons for this pattern of mate choice. [2]
  5. Evaluate the hypothesis that boldness is more important than colour when females choose mates. [2]

Body size in marine iguanas

6 marks

Marine iguanas feed on algae on rocky shores of the Galapagos Islands. Males defend territories where females come to mate. The body length (snout to vent) of adults was measured on two islands. On Genovesa there is much less algae than on Santa Fe. Error bars show ± 1 SD.

  1. Compare the body length of males and females. [2]
  2. Calculate how much longer, as a percentage, males on Santa Fe are than males on Genovesa. [1]
  3. Suggest why males are larger than females. [2]
  4. Suggest why both sexes are smaller on Genovesa. [1]

Practical

Modelling natural selection with beak tools

Model35 minD4.1.3, D4.1.5

You will need: Forceps, spoons and clothes pegs as beaks, seeds or beads of different sizes, cups, timer

  1. Each student gets one tool, and collects food for 30 seconds
  2. Only those who gather enough food survive to the next round
  3. Survivors pass on their tool type, and the round is repeated
  4. Graph how tool numbers change, and link this to selection

Safety: Keep beads and seeds off the floor to avoid slips.

Carry this out with your teacher's supervision and follow your school's lab rules.

Observations

Theory of knowledge

How do we test theories about long-term processes?

Evolution usually acts over long periods. Consider how far short-term observations can support claims about deep time.
