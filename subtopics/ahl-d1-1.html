How DNA is copied

D1.1.6

DNA polymerases work 5′ to 3′

pp. 608–609

DNA polymerase joins the 5′ phosphate of an incoming nucleotide to the free 3′ -OH at the end of the growing strand, using energy from the nucleotide’s extra phosphates. So new strands grow only 5′ to 3′, and the polymerase needs an existing 3′ end, a primer, to start.

D1.1.7

Leading and lagging strands

pp. 609–611

Because the strands are antiparallel, one new strand, the leading strand, is made continuously towards the fork from a single primer. The other, the lagging strand, is made discontinuously away from the fork as short Okazaki fragments, each needing its own primer.

D1.1.8

The enzymes

pp. 610–612

In prokaryotes, helicase separates the strands; DNA primase makes short RNA primers; DNA polymerase III extends them with DNA nucleotides; DNA polymerase I removes the RNA primers and replaces them with DNA; and DNA ligase joins the fragments into a continuous strand.

D1.1.9

Proofreading

pp. 613–614

If DNA polymerase III adds a nucleotide with a mismatched base, it removes it from the 3′ end and adds the correct one. Proofreading cuts the error rate about a hundredfold, keeping mutations rare. Taq polymerase, used in PCR, cannot proofread.

Practical

Modelling leading and lagging strands

Model30 minD1.1.6, D1.1.7, D1.1.8

You will need: Long paper strips marked 5' and 3', short primer strips, cards for each enzyme

  1. Separate the strands at a moving replication fork
  2. Build the leading strand continuously in the 5' to 3' direction
  3. Build the lagging strand in short fragments, adding primers each time
  4. Use enzyme cards to show what each enzyme does

Safety: No practical hazards.

Carry this out with your teacher's supervision and follow your school's lab rules.
