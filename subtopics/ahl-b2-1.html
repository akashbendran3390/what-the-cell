Membrane fluidity

B2.1.11

Fatty acids and fluidity

p. 237

Saturated fatty acid tails are straight and pack closely, so they make a membrane less fluid. Unsaturated tails are kinked at their double bonds, cannot pack closely and keep the membrane fluid at lower temperatures. Membranes must be fluid enough for proteins to move and work, but not so fluid that they leak. Organisms such as the lake sturgeon raise the share of unsaturated fatty acids when the water gets colder: homeoviscous adaptation.

B2.1.12

Cholesterol

pp. 237–238

In animal membranes, cholesterol sits with its polar -OH group next to the phosphate heads and its non-polar rings and tail among the fatty acid tails. It acts as a fluidity buffer: at low temperature it keeps phospholipids apart so the membrane stays fluid; at high temperature it restricts their movement so the membrane is less fluid and less leaky.

B2.1.13

Vesicles form and fuse

pp. 238–239

Because phospholipids can flow, a membrane can pinch inwards to make a vesicle (endocytosis: phagocytosis of solids, pinocytosis of liquids) and a vesicle can fuse with the plasma membrane (exocytosis) without leaving a hole. Both need ATP. Macrophages engulf old red blood cells; plasma cells secrete antibodies and neurons release neurotransmitters by exocytosis.

Channels, pumps and cotransporters

B2.1.14

Gated ion channels

pp. 239–241

Gated channels open and close, letting ions diffuse down their gradients. Voltage-gated Na+ and K+ channels in axons open when the membrane potential changes; a K+ channel is then plugged by a “ball and chain” until the membrane repolarizes. Neurotransmitter-gated channels, such as the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor, open when acetylcholine binds, letting Na+ into the post-synaptic cell.

B2.1.15

The sodium-potassium pump

pp. 241–242

An exchange transporter. Three Na+ bind inside; ATP phosphorylates the pump, which opens outwards and releases them; two K+ bind outside; the phosphate leaves and the pump opens inwards, releasing K+. Both ions move against their gradients, building the gradients used by neurons and cotransporters.

B2.1.16

Indirect active transport of glucose

p. 242

In the small intestine and kidney tubules, Na+/K+ pumps keep Na+ low inside the cell. Na+ diffuses back in through a sodium-dependent glucose cotransporter, carrying glucose with it (two Na+ per glucose), even against the glucose gradient. The cotransporter uses no ATP itself, so this is indirect active transport. Glucose then leaves the cell by facilitated diffusion into the blood.

Tissues

B2.1.17

Cell adhesion

p. 243

Cell-adhesion molecules (CAMs) are membrane proteins that bind cells to each other (usually cadherins) or to the extracellular matrix (usually integrins). They form junctions: tight junctions seal epithelia, anchoring junctions give strength and gap junctions link the cytoplasm of neighbouring cells. Plant cells rely instead on interlocking walls and plasmodesmata.

Temperature and membrane permeability

Lab50 minB2.1.11, B2.1.12

You will need: Beetroot cylinders, water baths from 20 to 70 C, test tubes, colorimeter or colour chart

  1. Rinse the beetroot until the water runs clear
  2. Leave equal pieces in water at each temperature for 20 minutes
  3. Compare the colour of the water
  4. Plot the results, and explain them using membrane fluidity

Safety: Take care with hot water and with cutting tools. Beetroot stains.

Nature of science and TOK

Nature of science

Experiments

In 1970 Frye and Edidin fused mouse and human cells, and saw their membrane proteins mix, giving strong evidence that membranes are fluid.

Theory of knowledge

A single clear experiment can be very persuasive. Consider what else would be needed before a scientific community accepts a claim.
