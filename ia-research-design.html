Stage 1: Choose your research question

Week 1 · Toddle Task 1 · Checkpoint 1

Your research question shapes every other mark. A narrow, measurable question about a biological phenomenon makes the method, analysis and conclusion straightforward.

Step 1: Pick an investigation type

Type You will… Biology examples
Laboratory experiment Change one variable and measure its effect Enzyme activity, osmosis, photosynthesis, respiration, microbial growth
Field study Sample organisms in their environment Quadrats or transects along a gradient (light, distance from water, trampling)
Database analysis Extract and analyse published biological data Gene or protein sequences, species distribution records, health data
Simulation or model Analyse outputs of a validated model Population genetics, predator–prey or epidemic models

Step 2: Build the question

Experiment: How does [independent variable, and how it is changed] across [range, number of levels, units]
affect [dependent variable], measured by [method/instrument, units], in [organism (Genus species) or biological system]?

Correlation or field study: What is the relationship between [variable 1, units] and [variable 2, units]
for [organism (Genus species)] at [type of site] during [season/month]?

Database: What is the relationship between [variable 1] and [variable 2] in [system], using [database] data from [date range]?

Step 3: Compare weak and strong questions

Weak Strong
Does temperature affect enzymes? How does temperature (20, 30, 40, 50, 60 °C) affect the rate of catalase activity in Solanum tuberosum tissue, measured by the volume of oxygen released from 3% hydrogen peroxide in 60 s?
Do plants grow better with more light? How does light intensity (5 levels, 50–250 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) affect the rate of photosynthesis of Cabomba caroliniana, measured by change in dissolved oxygen over 10 min?
What is the effect of salt on potatoes? How does sucrose concentration (0.0–1.0 mol dm⁻³ in 0.2 steps) affect the percentage change in mass of Solanum tuberosum cylinders after 24 h?
Does light affect plants in the forest? What is the relationship between light intensity (lux) and percentage cover of Pteridium aquilinum along a woodland transect in the dry season?

Step 4: Check it is feasible

Database tip. Broad indicators such as national development or wealth indices give weak, messy data. Choose specific biological variables.

Stage 2: Learn from mentor IAs

Week 2 · Toddle Task 2 (repeated in class at Stages 6, 10 and 11)

The fastest way to learn what moderators reward is to mark real Biology IAs yourself. You will do this four times, once for each criterion, just before you write that section of your own report.

Session When You mark Then you write
Research design Week 2 Question, context, method Your 25% draft
Data analysis Week 6 Tables, graphs, uncertainties, statistics Your analysis
Conclusion Week 8 Five sample conclusions, ranked Your conclusion
Evaluation Week 8 Five sample evaluations, ranked Your evaluation

How each session works (30 minutes)

  1. Read and annotate on your own (10 min). For each rubric aspect, decide a band and note the evidence. Is it stated, described or explained? What is missing?

  2. Agree as a group (10 min). Agree a group mark or ranking. Write 3–5 sentences giving the band for each aspect, quoting evidence, naming one strength and one flaw that decided it.

  3. Score reveal (10 min). Your teacher shares the moderator’s mark and comments. Where did you differ, and why?

What to look for

  • Research design: a specific question with units, method and Genus species; a context narrowed to the variables; every methodological choice justified; a method you could repeat.

  • Data analysis: titles, headers, units, consistent decimal places; uncertainties in headers; error bars defined; statistics chosen for a reason; worked calculations.

  • Conclusion: answers the question with real values; avoids proves; uses SD, error-bar overlap and p-values; explains the biology and compares with published work.

  • Evaluation: weaknesses tied to specific apparatus, biological assumptions or steps, not “human error”; explains which way each flaw biased the data; realistic, detailed improvements.

Stage 3: Research design — the 25% draft

Week 3 · Toddle Task 3 · Checkpoint 2

This draft builds Research design. It is your planned method; you will update it later to match what you actually did. The top band needs every decision explained, so keep asking: why this choice?

3.1 Research question

State it at the top of the report, using the Stage 1 checklist.

3.2 Scientific context (description of the system)

  • Name the biological topic or phenomenon (for example, enzyme denaturation, osmosis, the light-dependent reactions).

  • Describe the relevant background biology, narrowed to your two variables, not the whole topic.

  • Explain how your variables relate to the biological system.

  • Add a figure (diagram or photo) if it helps explain a mechanism.

  • Cite academic papers or textbooks for every biological claim.

3.3 Theory of direct relevance and hypothesis

  • Explain the known effect of your independent variable on your dependent variable and the biological mechanism behind it.

  • Write a prediction of the trend you expect, justified by that mechanism. Do not write it as null/alternative hypotheses; those come in Stage 8.

  • Describe the expected pattern (for example, “rate increases to an optimum near 40 °C, then falls as the enzyme denatures”).

  • Cite sources for the hypothesis.

3.5 Apparatus, materials and uncertainties

  • List every item with brand, size and quantity. Give concentrations (mol dm⁻³, g dm⁻³ or %) and volumes (cm³) or masses (g) for every chemical and biological reagent; dimensions for containers. Metric units only.

  • Add a labelled sketch or photo of the set-up.

Instrument Quantity measured Uncertainty (±, units) How the uncertainty was decided

Deciding uncertainty: analogue scales, usually ± half the smallest division; digital readouts, ± the last digit; or the manufacturer’s value. Hand timing: allow for reaction time.

3.6 Justification of methods

  • Describe any pilot trial and how it improved the method.

  • Justify the choice of organism or tissue: why is it suitable?

  • Explain instrument choices where options exist (pH probe vs pH paper; colorimeter vs colour chart).

  • Explain why random sampling is needed and how you will do it.

  • Cite any standard protocol you followed.

3.7 Procedure

  • Numbered steps in logical order, detailed enough for someone else to repeat exactly.

  • Include how you change the independent variable, measure the dependent variable, maintain and measure controls, sample randomly, take repeats and record qualitative observations.

  • Refer to annotated diagrams or photos in the relevant steps.

  • Database or simulation: the input selections and the criteria used to extract data.

Stage 4: Plan and collect your data

Weeks 3–5

Biological material is unpredictable: seeds fail, cultures contaminate, enzymes lose activity. Plan for problems, start early, and record everything at the moment you measure it.

4.1 Make your data collection plan (30 minutes)

Write 5–7 sentences answering:

  • When will you get your materials and organisms?

  • When and where will you set up and run the investigation?

  • How many sessions will you need, and how long is each?

  • What is your back-up if something fails? (If you are growing plants or cultures, start now.)

  • One question you still have about your data collection.

The four requirements of data collection

  1. Enough data: at least 5 levels and at least 5 trials per level (or 20+ data pairs).

  2. Quantitative and qualitative data.

  3. Uncertainty and consistent digits: every value to the precision of its instrument.

  4. Data for every variable: independent, dependent and controlled, plus photos.

4.2 Part A: Photos

Photograph (without showing your face): the materials; you carrying out the investigation; the independent variable; the dependent variable; the control of variables. Caption each photo to explain what it shows and how it relates to your design. Database or simulation studies: use screenshots.

4.3 Part B: Measurement uncertainty

Tool for the independent variable
Tool for the dependent variable
Tool(s) for controlled variables

Be realistic. A stopwatch reads to ±0.01 s, but reaction time is about ±0.2 s. A tape measure on uneven ground is less precise than its markings. Counts (colonies, stomata, organisms in a quadrat) may need an uncertainty based on how much two counters disagree. Databases: report the stated uncertainty, or explain that an unknown uncertainty is itself a limitation.

4.5 Part D: Qualitative data

Record observations for each level: colour of tissue or indicator, bubbling, turgidity, contamination, leaf condition, weather and site conditions. Use annotated photos, sketches or maps. Database studies: state that no qualitative data was available.

4.6 Method change log

Date What changed Why Effect on data

Collaboration: you may collect data with others, but your write-up and analysis must be your own; state which data was shared. Anomalies: never delete a strange value. Flag it, note what happened, and deal with it in your analysis.
