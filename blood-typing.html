What is the patient’s blood group?

How the test works

Red blood cells carry antigens on their surface: antigen A, antigen B, both or neither, and the Rh antigen D (Rh positive) or not (Rh negative). Each test serum contains antibodies against one antigen. Antibodies have two binding sites, so each one can stick to two cells at once and link many cells into clumps.

The rule

The recipient’s plasma contains antibodies against any A or B antigen their own cells lack. If the donor’s red cells carry one of those antigens, the antibodies clump them, which can block small blood vessels. So a donor’s cells must not carry an antigen the recipient lacks. Rh-negative people make anti-D once they have been exposed to Rh-positive blood, so they should receive Rh-negative blood. Group O negative cells carry none of these antigens, so they can be given to anyone in an emergency.

Key idea

The ABO gene has three alleles. A person with group A could be IAIA or IAi, and group B could be IBIB or IBi, so two parents with groups A and B can have a child of any group. Group O (ii) needs an i allele from each parent. Group AB (IAIB) cannot have a group O child. The Genetics Lab has a full Punnett grid tool.
