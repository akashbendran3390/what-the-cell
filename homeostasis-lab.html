Keeping the inside steady

Your body keeps blood glucose, core temperature and blood water content close to a set point. In this lab you push each one away from normal – with a meal, a cold day, a run or a salty snack – and watch the receptors, control centre and effectors bring it back by negative feedback. Then you break the system (diabetes, switched-off responses) to see why each part matters.

What is happening?

The hypothalamus compares blood temperature with the set point (about 37 °C) and sends nerve signals to the effectors.

Thyroxine raises the metabolic rate in long cold spells; brown adipose tissue in babies releases heat because its mitochondria let protons leak back without making ATP.

Syllabus: D3.3.5, D3.3.6

Try these

  1. Snowy day: list the responses that switch on, in the order they appear.
  2. Switch off shivering on the snowy day. What happens to core temperature?
  3. Hot day, can't sweat: why does core temperature keep rising even with wide arterioles?
  4. Marathon: why do sweating and wide arterioles appear even though the air is cool?
  5. Explain why this is negative feedback, using the words receptor, hypothalamus and effector.

What is happening?

Time-Blood solute concentration-ADH released-Aquaporins in duct membranes-Urine made-Urine concentration-

Osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus sense the solute concentration of the blood. ADH from the posterior pituitary makes collecting duct cells put more aquaporins in their membranes, so more water is reabsorbed into the salty medulla.

Syllabus: D3.3.7, D3.3.10 (HL)

Try these

  1. Drink a litre. How do ADH, urine volume and urine colour change, and how long does it take?
  2. Run on a hot day. Why does urine become dark and small in volume?
  3. Turn on "No ADH made" and drink. Why is this person always thirsty?
